# Urban Fantasy / Hidden Powers Genre Template

> **Core selling point**: familiar modern life, invaded by the
> extraordinary. The appeal is finding the uncanny inside the mundane, not
> escaping the mundane entirely.

## 1. The three eras of disclosure (pick one as the project's starting state)

### Hidden era
- **Status quo**: very few people have powers; ordinary people don't know
  the supernatural exists; any power-user is either hiding or officially
  controlled.
- **Conflict shape**: protagonist hides their nature while dodging both
  authorities and antagonists who already know.
- **Tone**: mystery, suppressed tension, urban legend.
- **Hard rule (plausibility patch, see below)**: any supernatural event an
  ordinary bystander could plausibly notice (an explosion, a building
  collapse, a public fight) needs an on-page "real-world aftermath +
  cover story" beat within this chapter or the next — skipping this is
  the single most common thing that makes an urban-fantasy setting feel
  unconvincing.

### Outbreak era
- **Status quo**: the supernatural is becoming impossible to hide; the
  old order is visibly cracking.
- **Conflict shape**: social upheaval, factions reshuffling, public
  disclosure forcing new institutions into existence.
- **Tone**: chaos, opportunity, high energy.

### New-order era
- **Status quo**: the supernatural is now normal, integrated into
  institutions and daily life.
- **Conflict shape**: larger-scale threats (a rival faction, an incursion)
  that only matter because the world has already adjusted to the
  supernatural being real.
- **Tone**: epic stakes, societal-scale conflict.

## 2. Faction dynamics

- **The official body** (a government agency, a licensing authority): the
  order-keeper — protagonist's relationship to it (recruited, hunted,
  begrudgingly cooperating) is a core early-plot decision.
- **Underground players**: money-driven organizations seeking to control
  or exploit power-users; cult-like factions serving something dangerous;
  mutual-aid guilds of power-users themselves.

## 3. Power-system categories (pick what fits, don't require all)

Give each protagonist and major rival an explicit category and tier — this
becomes a story-bible world-element note others' capabilities get checked
against:
- Elemental/output-focused (high damage, resource-limited)
- Enhancement (strength/speed/regeneration — favors sustained fights)
- Mental (harder to depict, easy to become a "win button" if not
  constrained — define hard limits explicitly)
- Exotic (space/time/causality-adjacent — establish narrow, specific
  limits at creation, since these are the easiest category to accidentally
  overpower)

## 4. Classic payoff patterns

- **The hidden-strength reveal**: protagonist dismissed as ordinary, then
  demonstrates overwhelming capability at a turning point — same
  requirement as progression-fantasy's version: the earlier dismissal has
  to be played straight.
- **The lucky find**: protagonist recognizes value in something experts
  overlooked, because their hidden knowledge/sense gives them an edge —
  works especially well combined with a mundane setting (a pawn shop, an
  antique market).
- **The protection assignment**: protagonist assigned to guard someone,
  forcing proximity that develops the relationship alongside the plot —
  a reliable structure for combining Quest and Fire strands.

## Entity types this genre typically needs story-bible notes for

The disclosure-era rule itself (world-element note, since it constrains
every subsequent chapter), major factions, the protagonist's power category
and its explicit limits.
