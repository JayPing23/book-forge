# Romance Genre Template

> **Core selling point**: the reader reads for the relationship's emotional
> arc — the Fire strand isn't a subplot here, it's the main plot. Plot
> events exist to create and release romantic tension, not the other way
> around.

## 1. Relationship arc structure (define explicitly in the outline)

- **Meet**: how the leads' first encounter creates friction or attraction
  (or both) — this sets the relationship's initial dynamic (rivals-to-lovers,
  forced-proximity, slow-burn-friends, etc.) and should be a deliberate
  choice stated in the volume outline, not an incidental scene.
- **Escalation beats**: a series of scenes that each raise emotional
  stakes — not just more time together, but each beat should change what
  the leads know or feel about each other. A relationship arc with no
  escalation beats reads as static regardless of how many scenes the leads
  share.
- **The turn/obstacle**: something threatens the relationship — external
  (circumstance, a rival, a secret) or internal (a flaw from a character's
  Motivation Core creating genuine incompatibility, not manufactured
  miscommunication for its own sake).
- **Resolution**: how the obstacle resolves, and what it costs or changes
  in each character.

## 2. Avoid the miscommunication trap

The single most common romance-genre failure mode: conflict manufactured by
a misunderstanding that either character's established personality
wouldn't actually sustain (a five-second conversation would resolve it, but
it doesn't happen for no in-character reason). The Motivation/Agency
Reviewer's "does this action trace to the character's motivation, not just
plot convenience" check is unusually load-bearing in this genre — a
prolonged miscommunication needs a real reason rooted in a character's
established fear or wound, not just narrative convenience.

## 3. Heat/tone level (state explicitly per project)

Define upfront and keep consistent: how explicit physical content gets,
whether scenes fade-to-black or continue, and how this interacts with the
platform convention (some platforms restrict explicit content; check this
before drafting, not after).

## 4. Classic payoff patterns

- **The reveal of feelings under pressure**: emotional stakes forced into
  the open by an external plot event, rather than a calm conversation —
  combines Quest and Fire strands efficiently.
- **The grand gesture**: a character acts against their own established
  caution/pride specifically because of what they've learned through the
  relationship — this only lands if the "against type" action is earned by
  the arc, not dropped in for the finale.
- **The parallel growth**: both leads change in ways that make them
  specifically compatible by the end, not just compatible because the plot
  says so — track this explicitly in each character's arc notes.

## Entity types this genre typically needs story-bible notes for

Both leads' Motivation Cores are especially load-bearing here (the whole
plot runs through them) — invest real detail at character creation. Any
rival/obstacle character needs their own real motivation too, not just
"exists to create conflict."
