# Progression Fantasy / Cultivation Genre Template

> **Core selling point**: visible, structured power growth. The reader
> reads for the next milestone as much as for the plot — progression is
> not a backdrop, it's a primary payoff mechanism.

## Optional creative-constraint pairing

Pair with a `depth_dial: popcorn` default unless the project explicitly
wants a slower-burn, more introspective take — this genre's core appeal
skews Quest-heavy by convention.

## 1. Power-system tiers

Define explicit, named tiers with a clear gap between each (the gap itself
is what makes a breakthrough satisfying — a smooth power curve reads as
weightless). Example skeleton, adapt names to the project's setting:

- **Tier 1 (Novice)**: barely above an ordinary person; every fight is real risk.
- **Tier 2 (Adept)**: consistent advantage over ordinary threats; still loses to Tier 3 without a trick.
- **Tier 3 (Expert)**: recognized authority; fights are now about strategy, not just raw stat comparison.
- **Tier 4 (Master)**: rare; can reshape local circumstances (a region, an organization) through personal power alone.
- **Tier 5+ (as the story needs)**: escalate only when the story's scope escalates with it — don't inflate tiers faster than the plot's stakes.

**Hard rule**: a character's tier is a fact on record in their story-bible
note. A sudden unexplained tier jump is exactly what the Continuity
Reviewer's world-rule check exists to catch — if a jump is earned, the
earning has to be on the page (a resource, a trial, a cost), not implied.

## 2. Faction dynamics

- **The established order** (a sect, academy, guild, corporation):
  gatekeeps resources and status; the protagonist's early relationship to
  it (outsider, low-ranked member, disgraced former member) sets up their
  climb.
- **Peer rivals**: characters at a similar tier, whose animosity or
  grudging respect tracks the protagonist's own progression — a rival who
  never grows becomes irrelevant; give them their own arc.
- **The looming threat**: an antagonist or faction operating at a tier the
  protagonist can't yet touch — this is what makes the tier system matter
  dramatically, not just numerically.

## 3. Classic payoff patterns (structural, not scene-for-scene)

- **The underestimated reveal**: protagonist's power is dismissed or
  hidden, then demonstrated at a moment that recontextualizes an earlier
  scene. Payoff requires the earlier dismissal to have been played
  straight, not winked at.
- **The breakthrough under pressure**: a tier advancement happens at the
  worst possible moment, tying power growth directly to stakes rather than
  training-montage downtime.
- **The rival becomes an ally (or vice versa)**: a relationship's power
  dynamic inverts as tiers shift — plan this in the outline's character-arc
  section, don't let it happen by accident.

## 4. Plausibility patch: progression pacing

The genre's biggest risk is progression that either stalls (reader
frustration) or races (stakes feel weightless). Volume outlines should
state an explicit progression checkpoint per volume — at least one
tier-relevant development (even a partial one, a new technique, a resource
gained) every volume, tracked in the Quest-strand percentage.

## Entity types this genre typically needs story-bible notes for

Power system rules (world-element note), major factions (world-element
notes), named techniques/abilities the protagonist and rivals use (track as
world-element notes if reused across chapters — a one-off technique doesn't
need its own note, a signature move a character returns to does).
