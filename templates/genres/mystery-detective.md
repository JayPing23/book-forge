# Mystery / Detective Genre Template

> **Core selling point**: the reader is playing a game with the author —
> the pleasure is in whether they can solve it before the reveal, and
> whether the solution feels earned in hindsight. This genre lives or dies
> on fairness to the reader, more than any other.

## 1. Pick a convention explicitly (this is a Research Agent craft-research
   decision, not a default) — and state which one in the project's outline

- **Fair-play whodunit**: every clue needed to solve the mystery is shown
  to the reader on the page, even if its significance isn't obvious until
  the reveal. The Thread-Ledger Reviewer's "unlogged setup" check is
  unusually strict here — every clue is a plot thread that must be logged
  the moment it's planted.
- **Hardboiled/noir**: the mystery is secondary to atmosphere and the
  investigator's moral compromise; fair-play clue discipline matters less
  than tone and voice consistency.
- **Cozy**: lower stakes, often a closed community of suspects, tone stays
  light even with a murder at the center; reader-fairness discipline is
  still expected but violence/darkness is kept off-page.
- **Procedural**: focus on the mechanics of investigation itself (forensics,
  interviews, process) rather than a single brilliant deduction.

## 2. The clue ledger (specific to this genre — beyond the normal plot-thread ledger)

Every clue gets its own plot-thread note, tagged distinctly from ordinary
plot threads, with:
- What it appears to mean on first read (the surface reading)
- What it actually means once solved (only fill in once the outline
  reaches the reveal)
- Which chapter plants it, which chapter (if any) a red herring
  misdirects around it, and which chapter resolves it

**Hard rule for fair-play**: if a piece of information is necessary to
solve the mystery, it must have a plot-thread note with an
`introduced_chapter` at or before the chapter where a character first
deduces something from it. A detective who "just knows" something the
reader was never shown is exactly the failure mode this genre can't
survive.

## 3. Suspect/red-herring design

- Give every viable suspect an actual motive and opportunity on record
  (story-bible character notes) — a suspect who's only there to be a red
  herring, with no real motive, reads as author manipulation rather than
  fair misdirection.
- At least one red herring should be resolved *before* the final reveal
  (not all misdirection should cash out simultaneously at the end) — this
  is a pacing convention, not a hard rule, but its absence is a common
  amateur-mystery tell.

## 4. Classic payoff patterns

- **The reread-worthy reveal**: the solution recontextualizes an earlier,
  seemingly-innocuous scene. This is the genre's signature payoff and
  depends entirely on the clue ledger discipline above.
- **The detective's blind spot**: the investigator's own flaw or bias
  (from their Motivation Core) causes them to miss something the reader
  could have caught — ties character depth directly into the mystery
  mechanics, works well at any Depth Dial setting.
- **The false solution**: an earlier, plausible-but-wrong solution gets
  disproven before the real one — raises stakes for the actual reveal.

## Entity types this genre typically needs story-bible notes for

Every suspect (with motive/opportunity/alibi on record), every clue (as a
specially-tagged plot-thread note), the specific convention chosen (as a
project-level note so the Outline-Adherence Reviewer knows what "on-plan"
means for this project).
